## Topic Section: 1.2 Expressing Concentration of Solutions (Syllabus Weight: 9.66% | Allocated Quota: 2)

---
### Q2 | Short Answer (SA) | 3 Marks | 🔴 Tier 1
- **Target Concept:** Temperature dependence of concentration terms (Molarity vs. Molality)
- **Question:**
  State the key difference between molarity and molality in terms of their dependence on temperature. Explain why one of them changes with temperature while the other remains unaffected.

- **Marking Scheme & Solution:**
  - **Definition / Distinction:** Molarity depends on volume, whereas molality depends on the mass of the solvent. — **[1 Mark]**
  - **Reason for Temperature Dependence:** Molarity involves the volume of the solution in the denominator ($\text{Molarity} = \frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Volume in L}}$). Since volume changes with a change in temperature (due to thermal expansion or contraction), molarity is a function of temperature. — **[1 Mark]**
  - **Reason for Temperature Independence:** Molality involves the mass of the solvent in the denominator ($\text{mol} \text{ kg}^{-1}$). Mass does not change with temperature; hence, molality is independent of temperature. — **[1 Mark]**

- **Why this Tier:** A standard conceptual board question frequently tested to evaluate the fundamental understanding of solution concentration parameters.
---

### Q3 | Long Answer (LA) / Derivation | 5 Marks | 🟡 Tier 2
- **Target Concept:** Interconversion of concentration terms (Mass percentage, Density, Molality, Molarity, and Mole Fraction)
- **Question:**
  Calculate (a) molality, (b) molarity, and (c) mole fraction of KI if the density of a 20\% (mass/mass) aqueous solution of KI is $1.202 \text{ g mL}^{-1}$. 
  *(Given atomic masses: $\text{K} = 39 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$, $\text{I} = 127 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$, $\text{H} = 1 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$, $\text{O} = 16 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$)*

- **Marking Scheme & Solution:**
  - **Preliminary Molar Mass & Basis Selection:**
    - Let the total mass of the solution be $100 \text{ g}$.
    - Mass of KI (solute) = $20 \text{ g}$, Mass of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (solvent) = $100 - 20 = 80 \text{ g}$.
    - Molar mass of KI $= 39 + 127 = 166 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$.
    - Moles of KI ($n_{\text{KI}}$) $= \frac{20 \text{ g}}{166 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.1205 \text{ mol}$.
    - Moles of water ($n_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$) $= \frac{80 \text{ g}}{18 \text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 4.444 \text{ mol}$. — **[1 Mark]**
  - **(a) Calculation of Molality ($m$):**
    - $\text{Molality} = \frac{\text{Moles of KI}}{\text{Mass of solvent in kg}} = \frac{0.1205 \text{ mol}}{0.080 \text{ kg}} = 1.506 \text{ mol kg}^{-1}$ (or $1.51 \text{ m}$). — **[1 Mark]**
  - **(b) Calculation of Molarity ($M$):**
    - Volume of solution ($V$) $= \frac{\text{Mass of solution}}{\text{Density}} = \frac{100 \text{ g}}{1.202 \text{ g mL}^{-1}} = 83.19 \text{ mL} = 0.08319 \text{ L}$.
    - $\text{Molarity} = \frac{\text{Moles of KI}}{\text{Volume of solution in L}} = \frac{0.1205 \text{ mol}}{0.08319 \text{ L}} = 1.448 \text{ mol L}^{-1}$ (or $1.45 \text{ M}$). — **[2 Marks]**
  - **(c) Calculation of Mole Fraction of KI ($x_{\text{KI}}$):**
    - $x_{\text{KI}} = \frac{n_{\text{KI}}}{n_{\text{KI}} + n_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}} = \frac{0.1205}{0.1205 + 4.444} = \frac{0.1205}{4.5645} = 0.0264$. — **[1 Mark]**

### Q4 | Objective — MCQ | 1 Mark(s) | 🔴 Tier 1
- **Target Concept:** Henry's Law and relation between $K_H$ value and gas solubility
- **Question:**
  Which of the following statements is correct regarding Henry's law constant ($K_\text{H}$)?
  
  (A) $K_\text{H}$ is a universal constant independent of the nature of the gas and solvent.\\
  (B) Higher the value of $K_\text{H}$ at a given pressure, lower is the solubility of the gas in the liquid.\\
  (C) $K_\text{H}$ decreases with an increase in temperature for all gases.\\
  (D) The unit of $K_\text{H}$ is $\text{atm}^{-1}$ or $\text{bar}^{-1}$.

- **Marking Scheme & Solution:**
  - **Correct Option:** (B) — **[1 Mark]**
  - **Explanation:** According to Henry's law, $p = K_\text{H}x$, which rearranges to $x = \frac{p}{K_\text{H}}$. Thus, mole fraction $x$ (representing solubility) is inversely proportional to $K_\text{H}$. Therefore, a higher $K_\text{H}$ value implies lower solubility of the gas in the liquid. Option (A) is incorrect because $K_\text{H}$ depends on the nature of the gas; (C) is incorrect because $K_\text{H}$ generally increases with temperature; and (D) is incorrect because the unit of $K_\text{H}$ is pressure units (e.g., bar or kbar).

- **Why this Tier:** This is a fundamental conceptual question frequently tested in board examinations regarding the physical significance of the Henry's law constant.

- **Marking Scheme & Solution:**

  - (b) Dissolution of a gas in a liquid is an exothermic process (heat is evolved). According to Le Chatelier's principle, an increase in temperature shifts the equilibrium in the backward direction, causing a decrease in solubility. — **[$1 \text{ mark}$]**
  Therefore, warm water contains lesser dissolved oxygen compared to cold water, making it harder for aquatic species to breathe; hence they are more comfortable in cold water. — **[$1 \text{ mark}$]**

- **Why this Tier:** This is a core conceptual board-staple that tests fundamental law recall and Le Chatelier's principle application to environmental phenomena.

### Q8 | Long Answer (LA) / Derivation | 5 Marks | 🔴 Tier 1

- **Question:**
  (a) Neon gas is bubbled through water at $298\text{ K}$ with a partial pressure of $1.5\text{ bar}$. If the Henry's law constant ($K_H$) for neon in water at $298\text{ K}$ is $144.97\text{ kbar}$, calculate the concentration of neon in terms of mole fraction in the aqueous solution. ($3 \text{ marks}$)
  (b) Why do scuba divers use air diluted with helium instead of compressed air at high underwater pressures? Explain based on Henry's law. ($2 \text{ marks}$)

- **Marking Scheme & Solution:**
  - (a) **Step 1:** State Henry's law formula relating partial pressure and mole fraction: 
    $$p = K_H \cdot x$$ — **[$0.5 \text{ mark}$]**
  - **Step 2:** Substitute the given values into the expression ($p = 1.5\text{ bar}$ and $K_H = 144.97 \times 10^3\text{ bar}$):
    $$x = \frac{p}{K_H} = \frac{1.5\text{ bar}}{144970\text{ bar}}$$ — **[$1 \text{ mark}$]**
  - **Step 3:** Compute final numerical value for mole fraction of Neon:
    $$x = 1.035 \times 10^{-5}$$ — **[$1.5 \text{ marks}$ ($1 \text{ mark}$ for calculation, $0.5 \text{ mark}$ for correct sig figs/units)]**
  - (b) **Explanation:** At high pressures underwater, scuba divers breathe air at high pressure, causing a higher concentration of atmospheric gases ($\text{N}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$) to dissolve in their blood in accordance with Henry's law. When they ascend to the surface quickly, pressure decreases, causing dissolved $\text{N}_2$ gas to form bubbles in the blood stream. This blocks capillaries, causing a painful and dangerous medical condition known as "bends". To avoid this, scuba diver tanks are diluted with helium (which has low solubility in blood). — **[$2 \text{ marks}$ ($1 \text{ mark}$ for mechanism via Henry's law, $1 \text{ mark}$ for consequence/bends & helium role)]**

### Q9 | Objective — MCQ | 1 Mark(s) | 🔴 Tier 1
- **Target Concept:** Raoult's Law and Vapour Phase Mole Fractions ($y_i$)
- **Question:**
  Two volatile liquids A and B are mixed in a closed container to form an ideal solution. The vapour pressure of pure liquid A ($p_A^0$) is $150\text{ mm Hg}$ and that of pure liquid B ($p_B^0$) is $300\text{ mm Hg}$. If the mole fraction of liquid B in the solution mixture is $0.4$, what is the mole fraction of component B in the vapour phase ($y_B$)?
  
  (A) $0.40$  
  (B) $0.57$  
  (C) $0.63$  
  (D) $0.28$

- **Marking Scheme & Solution:**
  - Calculation of total vapour pressure: $p_{\text{total}} = p_A^0 + (p_B^0 - p_A^0)x_B = 150 + (300 - 150) \times 0.4 = 150 + 60 = 210\text{ mm Hg}$. — **[$1/2$ M]**

  - Calculation of vapour phase mole fraction ($y_B$): $y_B = \frac{p_B}{p_{\text{total}}} = \frac{120}{210} \approx 0.57$. — **[$1/4$ M]**
  - **Correct Option:** (B)

- **Why this Tier:** Tests the crucial board concept connecting liquid mole fraction, total pressure, and vapour-phase composition.

### Q11 | Long Answer (LA) / Derivation | 5 Mark(s) | 🔴 Tier 1
- **Target Concept:** Mathematical derivation of total vapour pressure equation and numerical application involving vapour phase composition
- **Question:**
  (a) Derive the mathematical expression for the total vapour pressure of an ideal binary solution containing two volatile liquids (component 1 and component 2) in terms of the mole fraction of one of the components ($x_2$). ($3\text{ Marks}$)  
  (b) Benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$) and Toluene ($\text{C}_7\text{H}_8$) form an ideal solution over the entire range of composition. The vapour pressure of pure benzene and pure toluene at $300\text{ K}$ are $50.71\text{ mm Hg}$ and $32.06\text{ mm Hg}$ respectively. Calculate the mole fraction of benzene in the vapour phase if $78\text{ g}$ of benzene is mixed with $92\text{ g}$ of toluene. ($2\text{ Marks}$)

- **Marking Scheme & Solution:**
  - **Part (a):**
    - State individual partial pressures using Raoult's Law: $p_1 = p_1^0 x_1$ and $p_2 = p_2^0 x_2$. — **[$1$ M]**
    - Apply Dalton's law of partial pressures: $p_{\text{total}} = p_1 + p_2 = p_1^0 x_1 + p_2^0 x_2$. — **[$1$ M]**
    - Substitute $x_1 = 1 - x_2$ to get: $p_{\text{total}} = p_1^0(1 - x_2) + p_2^0 x_2 = p_1^0 + (p_2^0 - p_1^0)x_2$. — **[$1$ M]**
  - **Part (b):**
    - Moles of benzene ($M = 78\text{ g mol}^{-1}$): $n_{\text{benzene}} = \frac{78\text{ g}}{78\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 1.0\text{ mol}$.  
      Moles of toluene ($M = 92\text{ g mol}^{-1}$): $n_{\text{toluene}} = \frac{92\text{ g}}{92\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 1.0\text{ mol}$.  
      Total moles = $2.0\text{ mol}$, so $x_{\text{benzene}} = 0.5$ and $x_{\text{toluene}} = 0.5$. — **[$1/2$ M]**
    - Calculate total vapour pressure:  
      $p_{\text{total}} = p_{\text{benzene}}^0 x_{\text{benzene}} + p_{\text{toluene}}^0 x_{\text{toluene}} = (50.71 \times 0.5) + (32.06 \times 0.5) = 25.355 + 16.03 = 41.385\text{ mm Hg}$. — **[$1/2$ M]**
    - Partial pressure of benzene: $p_{\text{benzene}} = 50.71 \times 0.5 = 25.355\text{ mm Hg}$. — **[$1/2$ M]**
    - Vapour phase mole fraction ($y_{\text{benzene}}$):  
      $y_{\text{benzene}} = \frac{p_{\text{benzene}}}{p_{\text{total}}} = \frac{25.355}{41.385} = 0.613$. — **[$1/2$ M]**

## Topic Section: 1.4.2 Raoult's Law as a Special Case of Henry's Law (Syllabus Weight: 2.53% | Allocated Quota: 1)

---
### Q12 | Short Answer (SA) | 3 Marks | 🔴 Tier 1
- **Target Concept:** Raoult's Law as a special case of Henry's Law
- **Question:**
  State Henry's law and Raoult's law for a volatile liquid component in a solution. By comparing their mathematical expressions, explain how Raoult's law can be considered a special case of Henry's law.

- **Marking Scheme & Solution:**
  - **Statement of Henry's Law:** The partial pressure of the gas in vapour phase ($p$) is proportional to the mole fraction of the gas ($x$) in the solution, given by $p = K_\text{H} x$. — **[1 Mark]**
  - **Statement of Raoult's Law:** The vapour pressure of a volatile component in a given solution is directly proportional to its mole fraction ($x_i$), given by $p_i = x_i p_i^0$ (where $p_i^0$ is the vapour pressure of pure component). — **[1 Mark]**
  - **Comparison and Conclusion:** Comparing both equations ($p = K_\text{H} x$ and $p_i = x_i p_i^0$), it is observed that both state that the partial pressure of a volatile component is directly proportional to its mole fraction in solution. Raoult's law becomes a special case of Henry's law when the Henry's law constant ($K_\text{H}$) becomes equal to the vapour pressure of the pure component ($p_i^0$). — **[1 Mark]**

- **Why this Tier:** This is a classic conceptual derivation frequently tested in board examinations to check the fundamental understanding of solution thermodynamics.

## Topic Section: 1.6.3 Depression of Freezing Point (Syllabus Weight: 7.51% | Allocated Quota: 2)

---
### Q17 | Short Answer (SA) | 3 Marks | 🔴 Tier 1
- **Target Concept:** Depression of freezing point and its relation to molality
- **Question:**
  When a non-volatile solute is added to a pure solvent, the freezing point of the solution decreases compared to that of the pure solvent. 
  (a) Define the freezing point of a substance in terms of vapour pressure. — **[1 Mark]**
  (b) Calculate the freezing point of an aqueous solution containing $18\text{ g}$ of glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) dissolved in $100\text{ g}$ of water. 
  *(Given: $K_{\text{f}}$ for water $= 1.86\text{ K kg mol}^{-1}$, Molar mass of glucose $= 180\text{ g mol}^{-1}$, Freezing point of pure water $= 273.15\text{ K}$)* — **[2 Marks]**

- **Marking Scheme & Solution:**
  - (a) The freezing point of a substance is defined as the temperature at which the vapour pressure of the substance in its liquid phase is equal to its vapour pressure in the solid phase. — **[1 Mark]**
  - (b) **Step 1:** Calculate the molality ($\text{m}$) of the solution:
    $$\text{Moles of glucose} = \frac{w_2}{M_2} = \frac{18\text{ g}}{180\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.1\text{ mol}$$
    $$\text{Mass of solvent } (w_1) = 100\text{ g} = 0.1\text{ kg}$$
    $$\text{Molality } (\text{m}) = \frac{0.1\text{ mol}}{0.1\text{ kg}} = 1.0\text{ mol kg}^{-1}$$ — **[1 Mark]**
  - **Step 2:** Calculate freezing point depression ($\Delta T_{\text{f}}$) and freezing point of the solution ($T_{\text{f}}$):
    $$\Delta T_{\text{f}} = K_{\text{f}} \times \text{m} = 1.86\text{ K kg mol}^{-1} \times 1.0\text{ mol kg}^{-1} = 1.86\text{ K}$$
    $$T_{\text{f}} = T_{\text{f}}^{\,0} - \Delta T_{\text{f}} = 273.15\text{ K} - 1.86\text{ K} = 271.29\text{ K}$$ — **[1 Mark]**

- **Why this Tier:** Standard board numerical testing the core application of the molal freezing point depression formula.

### Q18 | Long Answer (LA) | 5 Marks | 🟡 Tier 2
- **Target Concept:** Colligative properties and determination of molar mass using cryoscopic constant relation
- **Question:**
  (a) State Raoult's law explanation for the depression in freezing point when a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a solvent. Explain why the freezing point of a solution is lower than that of the pure solvent with the help of vapour pressure concepts. — **[2 Marks]**
  (b) Derive the mathematical expression relating the depression in freezing point ($\Delta T_{\text{f}}$) to the molar mass ($M_2$) of a non-volatile solute. — **[2 Marks]**
  (c) An organic substance weighing $2.5\text{ g}$ was dissolved in $80\text{ g}$ of benzene. The depression in freezing point of benzene was found to be $0.45\text{ K}$. Calculate the molar mass of the solid substance. *(Given: $K_{\text{f}}$ for benzene $= 5.12\text{ K kg mol}^{-1}$)* — **[1 Mark]**

- **Marking Scheme & Solution:**
  - (a) According to Raoult's law, adding a non-volatile solute lowers the vapour pressure of the solution. Since the solid phase and liquid phase are in dynamic equilibrium at the freezing point, the lower vapour pressure of the solution becomes equal to that of the solid solvent at a lower temperature compared to the pure solvent, resulting in a decrease in the freezing point. — **[2 Marks]**
  - (b) **Derivation:**
    1. Depression in freezing point ($\Delta T_{\text{f}}$) for dilute solutions is directly proportional to molality ($\text{m}$):
       $$\Delta T_{\text{f}} \propto \text{m} \implies \Delta T_{\text{f}} = K_{\text{f}}\text{m}$$ — **[1/2 Mark]**
    2. Molality ($\text{m}$) is defined as the number of moles of solute ($w_2/M_2$) per kilogram of solvent ($w_1/1000$):
       $$\text{m} = \frac{w_2 / M_2}{w_1 / 1000} = \frac{w_2 \times 1000}{M_2 \times w_1}$$ — **[1/2 Mark]**
    3. Substituting $\text{m}$ into the proportionality equation:
       $$\Delta T_{\text{f}} = \frac{K_{\text{f}} \times w_2 \times 1000}{M_2 \times w_1}$$ — **[1/2 Mark]**
    4. Rearranging for molar mass ($M_2$):
       $$M_2 = \frac{K_{\text{f}} \times w_2 \times 1000}{\Delta T_{\text{f}} \times w_1}$$ — **[1/2 Mark]**
  - (c) **Calculation:**
    $$M_2 = \frac{5.12\text{ K kg mol}^{-1} \times 2.5\text{ g} \times 1000\text{ g kg}^{-1}}{0.45\text{ K} \times 80\text{ g}} = \frac{12800}{36} = 355.56\text{ g mol}^{-1}$$ — **[1 Mark]**

- **Why this Tier:** Combines conceptual reasoning, mathematical derivation of a key colligative formula, and numerical calculation required for senior secondary board exams.

### Q20 | Long Answer (LA) / Derivation | 5 Marks | 🔴 Tier 1
- **Target Concept:** Osmotic pressure relation with molar mass and its experimental superiority over other colligative properties.
- **Question:**
  (a) Define osmotic pressure. Why is osmotic pressure considered a colligative property?  *(2 Marks)*  
  (b) Starting from the experimental observation that osmotic pressure ($\Pi$) is proportional to the molarity ($C$) of the solution at temperature $T$, derive the expression for calculating the molar mass ($M_2$) of a non-volatile solute. State the terms involved in the final expression.  *(3 Marks)*

- **Marking Scheme & Solution:**
  - (a) **Definition:** The excess pressure that must be applied to a solution to prevent osmosis (i.e., to stop the passage of solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane into the solution).  — **[1 Mark]**  
    - **Colligative Property Reason:** It depends on the number of solute particles (moles) present in the solution and not on their chemical identity.  — **[1 Mark]**  
  - (b) **Derivation:**  
    - Experimentally, $\Pi \propto C \times T$, where $C$ is molarity.  
      $$\Pi = C R T$$  — **[1/2 Mark]**  
    - Since molarity $C = \frac{n_2}{V}$ (where $V$ is volume in litres and $n_2$ is moles of solute), we substitute $C$:  
      $$\Pi = \left(\frac{n_2}{V}\right) R T \implies \Pi V = n_2 R T$$  — **[1 Mark]**  
    - Substituting $n_2 = \frac{w_2}{M_2}$ (where $w_2$ is mass of solute in grams and $M_2$ is molar mass):  
      $$\Pi V = \frac{w_2 R T}{M_2} \implies M_2 = \frac{w_2 R T}{\Pi V}$$  — **[1 Mark]**  
    - **Nomenclature/Terms:** $M_2 = \text{molar mass of solute}$, $w_2 = \text{given mass of solute}$, $R = \text{gas constant}$, $T = \text{temperature in Kelvin}$, $\Pi = \text{osmotic pressure}$, $V = \text{volume of solution in litres}$.  — **[1/2 Mark]**

- **Why this Tier:** Standard core board derivation that tests foundational theoretical understanding and mathematical manipulation of colligative properties.
